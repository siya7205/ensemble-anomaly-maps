# Dynamic Hotspot Detection in Molecular Dynamics: A Thesis Methodology

## Executive Summary

This document presents a comprehensive academic analysis of an end-to-end machine learning pipeline designed to detect residue-level dynamic hotspots in molecular dynamics trajectories. The methodology synthesizes time-lagged independent component analysis (tICA), Markov state modeling (MSM), and multi-signal anomaly detection to identify protein residues exhibiting kinetically unusual, structurally outlying, or functionally significant dynamics. Unlike traditional structural bioinformatics approaches that focus on static geometric features, this pipeline treats anomaly as fundamentally temporal and kinetic rather than merely structural.

---

## 1. Problem Framing from Code Implementation

The pipeline's architecture reveals an implicit definition of "dynamic hotspot" that transcends conventional structural biology characterizations. Rather than defining hotspots as simply flexible or strained regions, the implementation encodes a multi-dimensional concept where anomaly manifests simultaneously across kinetic, geometric, and energetic axes. This framing emerges from the three-signal fusion strategy implemented in `scoring/anomaly_v2.py`, which computes rarity (kinetic-thermodynamic), transition surprise (kinetic-barrier), and local density (geometric-structural) scores independently before combining them through robust rank-based normalization.

The treatment of anomaly as a temporal and kinetic concept rather than a purely structural one is evident in the pipeline's hierarchical organization. The feature extraction stage (`features/compute_md_features.py`) begins with structural descriptors—backbone dihedral angles (φ, ψ encoded as sin/cos pairs to preserve periodicity), Cα-Cα distances capturing tertiary contacts, radius of gyration reflecting compactness, and RMSD measuring global structural deviation. However, these geometric features serve merely as inputs to the subsequent kinetic modeling stages. The critical transformation occurs in the tICA projection, where features are reweighted according to their contribution to slow collective motions, explicitly filtering out fast, statistically irrelevant fluctuations.

This kinetic reframing is further reinforced by the MSM construction, which discretizes the continuous conformational landscape into metastable states characterized not by geometric similarity alone but by kinetic accessibility. Two conformations may be geometrically similar yet belong to different kinetic basins if separated by high free energy barriers. Conversely, geometrically distinct conformations may be kinetically equivalent if rapidly interconverting. The MSM's stationary distribution π quantifies thermodynamic equilibrium populations, while the transition matrix P encodes barrier heights and kinetic pathways. The pipeline's anomaly scores therefore identify not merely unusual structures but unusual kinetic behaviors: rare equilibrium states (high rarity = 1 - π[state]), kinetically forbidden transitions (high surprise = -log P[s_t → s_{t+lag}]), and structurally isolated conformations that the protein visits transiently (high local density score based on k-nearest neighbor distances in tICA space).

This kinetic perspective has profound implications for biological interpretation. A residue with high anomaly score is not simply "flexible" or "strained" but rather exhibits one or more of the following: occupation of rarely visited thermodynamic states, involvement in rare barrier-crossing transitions, or adoption of conformations geometrically dissimilar to the equilibrium ensemble. Such residues are candidates for functional hotspots precisely because function often requires sampling rare states (enzyme active sites during catalysis), traversing high barriers (allosteric switches), or accessing cryptic conformations (transient binding pockets).

## 2. Feature Design and Physical Interpretability

The feature extraction module (`features/compute_md_features.py`) implements a deliberate hierarchy from atomic coordinates to physically interpretable descriptors. The choice of backbone dihedral angles (φ, ψ) as primary features reflects deep domain knowledge: these angles represent the natural internal degrees of freedom for protein backbone motion, are rotationally and translationally invariant, and directly determine secondary structure. The sin/cos encoding (lines 64-76) addresses the periodicity problem—an angle of -179° and +179° differ by only 2° but naively differ by 358° numerically. This encoding ensures that geometrically similar conformations remain numerically proximate in feature space, a prerequisite for meaningful distance-based clustering in later stages.

The inclusion of Cα-Cα contact distances captures non-local tertiary structure information that dihedral angles alone cannot represent. The implementation computes all pairwise distances for Cα atoms (lines 43-49) but filters to "native contacts" defined as pairs within 8 Å in the reference structure. This threshold reflects the typical range of stabilizing non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, salt bridges, van der Waals contacts) in folded proteins. By tracking how these native contacts break and reform, the features implicitly encode folding/unfolding events, domain rearrangements, and quaternary structure changes.

Global features (RMSD and radius of gyration) provide complementary information at different length scales. RMSD captures overall structural similarity to a reference, useful for identifying large-scale conformational changes. Radius of gyration Rg = √(Σ_i m_i r_i² / Σ_i m_i) measures protein compactness, increasing when the protein unfolds or expands and decreasing when it collapses. These global features prevent the pipeline from missing coherent collective motions that may not manifest strongly in local dihedral changes.

The feature design embeds several critical assumptions. First, the pipeline assumes that backbone geometry (dihedrals + Cα distances) sufficiently represents functionally relevant dynamics. This is generally valid for proteins where function arises from backbone rearrangements (domain motions, loop closures, allosteric transitions) but may miss side-chain-driven processes like rotamer switching or metal coordination. Second, the native contact definition assumes that the reference structure is biologically relevant, which may fail for intrinsically disordered regions or alternative functional states not present in the starting structure. Third, the choice to average dihedral angle sin/cos values (lines 65-72) rather than treating each residue's φ, ψ separately reduces dimensionality but loses residue-specific information, trading granularity for statistical power.

An important implementation detail is the error handling for dihedral computation failures (lines 54-62). The try-except block catches ValueError, RuntimeError, and KeyError exceptions that arise when the topology lacks necessary atom types or when residues at chain termini cannot form valid φ/ψ angles. Rather than crashing, the code falls back to zero-valued placeholders. While pragmatic, this choice means that poorly defined or incomplete residues contribute null information, potentially biasing anomaly scores away from termini or unstructured regions where such failures are common.

The feature matrix dimensionality is F = 6 + N_contacts, where N_contacts scales as O(N_residues²) for all-vs-all distances but is typically limited to ~100-500 native contacts in practice. For a 200-residue protein, this yields F ≈ 300-600 features, a manageable dimensionality for tICA while retaining rich structural information. The subsequent tICA projection to d = 2-10 dimensions achieves a compression ratio of 30-300×, focusing on slow modes while discarding fast noise.

## 3. Kinetic Modeling: tICA, MSMs, and Validation

The pipeline's kinetic modeling infrastructure consists of three interconnected stages: model selection via VAMP-2 scoring, MSM construction with reversibility constraints, and multi-level validation. The implementation in `msm/select_lag_and_dim.py` performs a grid search over lag times τ ∈ {5, 10, 15, 20, 30, 50} frames and dimensions d ∈ {2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10}. This parameterization reflects the tension between capturing slow dynamics (requiring large τ and d) and maintaining statistical reliability (requiring small τ and d relative to trajectory length T).

The VAMP-2 score serves as the model selection criterion (lines 29-87). VAMP-2 measures how well the tICA components preserve time-lagged correlations in held-out validation data, providing a rigorous variational bound on the quality of the implied kinetic model. Mathematically, the score is Σ_i σ_i², where σ_i are the singular values of the normalized time-lagged correlation matrix. High VAMP-2 scores indicate strong separation of timescales, meaning the selected slow modes truly capture long-lived dynamics rather than noise. The implementation uses a 20% validation split (line 44) to prevent overfitting: tICA is fit on the training set and scored on the validation set, ensuring that selected parameters generalize beyond the training data.

The tICA dimension selection embeds a critical trade-off. Too few dimensions (d = 2-3) risk missing important slow modes, particularly in multi-domain proteins or allosteric systems where multiple independent slow processes coexist. Too many dimensions (d > 10) include progressively faster modes that may not satisfy the Markov assumption at the chosen MSM lag time, violating the theoretical foundations of MSM construction. The grid search resolves this trade-off empirically: the dimension yielding maximum VAMP-2 score optimally balances timescale separation against overfitting.

Lag time selection similarly navigates competing constraints. The Markov assumption underlying MSMs requires that dynamics within a lag time τ_MSM equilibrate, meaning transitions between states at time t and t + τ_MSM depend only on the state at time t, not on earlier history. If τ_MSM is too small, memory effects persist and the MSM becomes invalid. If τ_MSM is too large, transition statistics become poor and rare transitions are undersampled. The implementation tests lags spanning two orders of magnitude (5-50 frames), allowing the VAMP-2 score to identify the optimal balance. In practice, τ_MSM typically falls around 30 frames for typical protein MD simulations with 2-10 ps timesteps, corresponding to 60-300 ps physical time, consistent with the timescale of local conformational relaxation.

MSM construction (`msm/bootstrap_msm.py`) proceeds through a multi-step pipeline. After transforming features through the selected tICA model (line 87), the code applies k-means clustering to discretize the continuous tICA space into n_clusters = 30 discrete states (line 91). This cluster count is chosen to balance state resolution (finer discretization reveals more kinetic detail) against statistical power (more states dilute transition counts). The implementation uses a minimum frames-per-cluster criterion of 100 (from validation thresholds), ensuring that each state is robustly sampled.

The MSM itself is fit using maximum likelihood estimation with a reversibility constraint (line 95). Reversibility enforces detailed balance: the equilibrium flux from state i to state j equals the reverse flux, π_i P_ij = π_j P_ji. This constraint is physically justified for equilibrium MD simulations and dramatically reduces the number of free parameters from O(n²) to O(n²/2), improving statistical robustness. The reversible MSM is estimated using the deeptime library's MaximumLikelihoodMSM, which solves a constrained optimization problem to find the maximum-likelihood transition matrix satisfying detailed balance.

The validation infrastructure (`msm/validation.py`) implements three complementary checks. The Chapman-Kolmogorov (CK) test (lines 23-79) validates the Markov property by comparing predicted transition probabilities P^k at lag k·τ against empirically estimated probabilities from direct trajectory analysis. The test computes the mean absolute error across all state pairs and lag multiples; passing requires error < 0.2, indicating that the MSM accurately captures multi-step transition dynamics. CK test failures typically indicate that the lag time is too short or that the state discretization is too coarse.

Implied timescales analysis (lines 82-132) examines how the slowest relaxation timescales τ_i = -τ_MSM / ln(λ_i), where λ_i are the eigenvalues of the transition matrix, vary with lag time. For a valid MSM, implied timescales should plateau at sufficiently large lags, indicating that the MSM has converged to the true long-time dynamics. The implementation tests convergence by computing the coefficient of variation (std/mean) of the slowest timescale across multiple lag times; CV < 0.2 indicates convergence. Failure to converge suggests that the slowest processes are not well-separated from faster dynamics, requiring either longer simulation times or different feature engineering.

Bootstrap uncertainty quantification (lines 138-322 in `bootstrap_msm.py`) addresses the statistical uncertainty inherent in finite-sampling MSMs. The implementation generates 100 bootstrap samples by resampling frames with replacement (line 209), refits the entire TICA-MSM pipeline for each sample, and accumulates distributions of stationary probabilities π and transition matrices P. The output includes confidence intervals (default 95%) for all MSM parameters, enabling assessment of which states and transitions are robustly determined versus which are artifacts of sampling noise. This uncertainty quantification is critical for downstream anomaly scoring: a residue showing high anomaly due to a poorly estimated rare transition is less trustworthy than one supported by narrow confidence intervals.

## 4. Signal Definitions and Physical Interpretation

The anomaly scoring module (`scoring/anomaly_v2.py`) computes five complementary signals, each targeting a distinct aspect of molecular dynamics. The implementation philosophy treats anomaly as a multi-faceted phenomenon that cannot be captured by any single metric, requiring signal fusion to achieve robust hotspot identification.

The rarity signal (lines 154-172) computes 1 - π[state] for each frame's assigned MSM state. Frames in rarely visited states receive high rarity scores, while frames in highly populated states receive low scores. This signal reflects a purely thermodynamic perspective: rare states have low equilibrium probability because they are energetically unfavorable or entropically constrained. High-rarity residues often correspond to strained conformations, partially unfolded states, or transition state ensembles along functional pathways. Importantly, rarity is a global property of the state, not a local property of the residue; the subsequent residue-level aggregation (implemented separately) must account for which residues contribute most to state definitions.

The transition surprise signal (lines 174-189) quantifies how unexpected observed transitions are given the MSM transition matrix. For each pair of states (s_t, s_{t+lag}), the code computes -log P[s_t → s_{t+lag}]. Rare transitions (small P) yield high surprise, while common transitions yield low surprise. This signal captures kinetic barriers: high surprise indicates that the protein crossed a significant free energy barrier, often associated with functional conformational changes. The epsilon parameter (1e-12) prevents logarithms of zero for forbidden transitions; such transitions receive maximum surprise scores. The physical interpretation is that residues involved in high-surprise transitions act as kinetic hinges or switches, gating access to alternative functional states.

The local density signal (lines 192-213) uses k-nearest neighbor distances in tICA space to identify geometric outliers. For each frame's tICA coordinates, the code finds the k = 20 nearest neighbors and computes the mean distance to them. Frames far from their neighbors (low local density) receive high anomaly scores. This signal is fundamentally geometric rather than kinetic: it identifies conformations that are structurally unusual regardless of their thermodynamic stability or kinetic accessibility. High local-density scores indicate that the protein has adopted a conformation rarely sampled by the equilibrium ensemble, potentially corresponding to cryptic pockets, alternative binding poses, or misfolded intermediates.

The energy stress signal (lines 216-253) aggregates per-residue contact energies to identify frames with high total energetic strain. The implementation supports three aggregation methods: sum (total energy), mean (average energy per residue), and top-k (average of the k highest-energy residues). The sum method emphasizes globally strained frames, the mean method normalizes for system size, and the top-k method focuses on localized energetic hotspots. Energetic strain often precedes structural changes, making this signal a leading indicator of conformational transitions. The knowledge-based potentials used (Miyazawa-Jernigan-like) are approximate, capturing pairwise residue interactions but neglecting solvent-mediated effects, electrostatic screening, and quantum mechanical contributions.

The pocket volatility signal (lines 256-285) tracks frame-to-frame changes in binding pocket volumes, identifying frames where pockets open, close, or deform rapidly. Computed as |V_t - V_{t-lag}|, this signal highlights transient pocket dynamics relevant for drug binding and allosteric regulation. Pockets that are stable yield low volatility, while cryptic pockets that appear and disappear yield high volatility. The biological significance is profound: many proteins exhibit transiently opening pockets that are absent in crystal structures but are druggable in dynamics, a phenomenon increasingly recognized in drug discovery.

Signal categorization reveals three classes: purely kinetic (rarity, transition surprise depend solely on MSM parameters), purely structural (local density depends solely on geometric configuration), and hybrid (energy stress and pocket volatility depend on both structure and kinetic sampling). This classification is critical for understanding signal redundancy and complementarity. Highly correlated signals provide redundant information and reduce the effective degrees of freedom in fusion, while weakly correlated signals capture orthogonal aspects of dynamics and enable comprehensive hotspot characterization.

## 5. Signal Fusion and Aggregation Strategies

The fusion methodology implemented in `scoring/anomaly_v2.py` (lines 288-324) represents a carefully designed compromise between statistical robustness and interpretability. The pipeline first normalizes each signal independently using rank-based scaling, then fuses normalized signals via median aggregation, and finally applies temporal smoothing to reduce frame-to-frame noise. Each design choice embeds specific assumptions and trade-offs.

Rank normalization (lines 20-42) transforms arbitrary-scaled signals into uniform [0, 1] distributions by sorting values and mapping them to their quantile positions. Unlike linear scaling (which is sensitive to outliers) or z-score normalization (which assumes Gaussian distributions), rank normalization is distribution-free and outlier-robust. The implementation assigns the minimum value rank 0 and the maximum value rank 1, with intermediate values interpolated linearly. This approach ensures that all signals contribute equally to the fused score regardless of their native scales or distributional shapes. However, rank normalization discards magnitude information: a signal with a narrow range of values contributes as much as one with a wide range, which may or may not be desirable depending on whether signal magnitude encodes meaningful information.

The choice between rank and quantile normalization (lines 45-68) offers an alternative trade-off. Quantile normalization clips extreme outliers by mapping the lower α-quantile (default 1%) to 0 and the upper (1-α)-quantile to 99% to 1, with values outside this range clipped to [0, 1]. This approach is more aggressive in outlier suppression than rank normalization, which always preserves the full range. The implementation defaults to rank normalization, reflecting a design philosophy that outliers may represent genuine rare events (e.g., transient cryptic pockets) rather than noise to be discarded.

Median fusion (lines 318-322) computes the element-wise median across normalized signals, producing a final score that is robust to single-signal failures or outliers. If one signal exhibits a spurious spike due to numerical instability or physical artifact, the median is unaffected as long as the majority of signals remain reasonable. This robustness is critical given the heterogeneity of signal types: kinetic signals depend on MSM quality, structural signals depend on sampling completeness, and energetic signals depend on force field accuracy. Alternative fusion strategies (mean, weighted average, learned weights) are implemented but not default, reflecting skepticism about the ability to assign meaningful weights without ground-truth labels.

The windowed smoothing step (lines 89-101) applies a median filter with window size 5 frames to the fused score, reducing temporal jitter while preserving sharp transitions. Median filtering is chosen over mean filtering because it preserves edges: a brief excursion to a high-anomaly state remains detectable, whereas mean filtering would smear it across neighboring frames. The window size (5 frames) is small enough to preserve temporal resolution but large enough to suppress single-frame noise, representing a 10-30 ps smoothing window for typical MD timesteps. This choice reflects the assumption that functionally relevant anomalies persist for at least a few tens of picoseconds, distinguishing them from thermal noise.

Frame-to-residue aggregation (implemented in `scoring/signals.py`, lines 525-570) faces a fundamental challenge: how to map temporal anomaly scores (which are properties of entire frames) to spatial hotspot scores (which are properties of individual residues). The implementation offers three strategies. Weighted-mean aggregation weights each frame's score by the residue's contribution to that frame's anomaly, operationalized through per-residue RMSF, tICA importance, or dihedral deviation. This approach assumes that residues driving anomalous conformations should receive higher scores, rewarding causal involvement. Max aggregation assigns each residue the maximum anomaly score achieved in any frame where that residue contributed, emphasizing the most extreme events. Mean aggregation computes average anomaly across contributing frames, providing a more conservative estimate less sensitive to transient spikes.

The design trade-offs are subtle. Weighted-mean aggregation depends critically on accurate contribution matrices, which are non-trivial to estimate. Using RMSF as a proxy assumes that flexible residues drive anomalies, which may fail for hinge residues that show small displacements but critical functional importance. Using tICA importance assumes that residues with high loadings on slow modes drive anomalies, which may miss fast, localized events. Max aggregation risks false positives from single anomalous frames, while mean aggregation risks false negatives by averaging out brief but important events. The implementation defaults to weighted-mean with RMSF weights, representing a middle ground between conservatism and sensitivity.

An important unresolved question evident in the code comments (lines 537-545) is the appropriate null model for significance testing. Without ground truth labels, there is no principled way to distinguish biologically significant hotspots from statistical fluctuations. The pipeline produces relative scores (which residues are most anomalous within this trajectory) but cannot assign absolute significance (is this residue's anomaly score high enough to warrant experimental follow-up). The documentation explicitly notes this limitation, recommending external validation through mutation studies, binding assays, or comparison with known functional sites.

## 6. Sensitivity Analysis and Uncertainty Quantification

The pipeline's design philosophy prioritizes transparency in uncertainty over false precision, implementing multiple layers of sensitivity analysis and hyperparameter robustness checks. The validation infrastructure (`msm/validation.py` and `tests/test_pipeline_edge_cases.py`) explicitly probes failure modes rather than assuming success.

The VAMP-2 grid search (`msm/select_lag_and_dim.py`) constitutes a systematic sensitivity analysis over the two most critical hyperparameters: tICA lag time and dimensionality. By testing all combinations and recording the full grid (not just the optimum), the implementation enables post-hoc assessment of parameter sensitivity. A flat VAMP-2 surface (many parameter combinations yielding similar scores) indicates robustness: results are insensitive to exact choices. A peaked surface (a single optimum surrounded by rapidly declining scores) indicates fragility: small parameter changes significantly affect results. The output file `vamp2_grid.csv` preserves this information, enabling researchers to assess whether the selected parameters represent a unique optimum or merely one point in a broad plateau.

Bootstrap uncertainty quantification (`msm/bootstrap_msm.py`) goes beyond point estimates to quantify the statistical uncertainty in MSM parameters. The 100 bootstrap iterations (line 204) resample frames with replacement, generating statistically independent MSM estimates. The resulting distributions of stationary probabilities π and transition matrices P reveal which states and transitions are robustly determined (narrow confidence intervals) versus which are artifacts of finite sampling (wide intervals). Critically, the implementation uses frame-level resampling (lines 46-48) rather than block resampling, which is appropriate for trajectories where successive frames are weakly correlated after the MSM lag time but may underestimate uncertainty in highly correlated trajectories.

The pipeline implements adaptive parameter scaling (`scoring/signals.py`, lines 110-157) to maintain robustness across trajectory sizes spanning five orders of magnitude (100 to 10⁷ frames). For small trajectories (<500 frames), conservative parameters are used: k-nearest neighbors is reduced to max(5, n_frames/20) to avoid overfitting, and MSM lag is capped at n_frames/10 to ensure sufficient transitions. For very large trajectories (>200,000 frames), the implementation uses subsampling to maintain computational tractability: k-NN fitting uses only 50,000 randomly selected frames (line 304) while querying all frames (line 311), reducing complexity from O(n²) to O(n·m) where m << n. This subsampling introduces approximation error but prevents computational failure, reflecting a pragmatic trade-off between accuracy and feasibility.

Edge case testing (`tests/test_pipeline_edge_cases.py`) systematically probes boundary conditions. The tests verify graceful degradation for pathological inputs: zero-variance features (lines 139-185), single-state trajectories (lines 191-258), disconnected MSM graphs (lines 191-258), and extreme numerical ranges (lines 300-471). Rather than crashing, the implementation falls back to conservative defaults: constant features are normalized to zero, single-state trajectories return uniform anomaly scores, and disconnected components are handled independently. This defensive design prevents cascading failures where one anomalous input corrupts all downstream outputs.

Hyperparameter impact is documented in configuration files and validation reports. The `configs/pipeline.yaml` file centralizes all tunable parameters with comments explaining their effects. The validation report (`validation_report.json`) includes correlation matrices showing how different anomaly signals covary, enabling assessment of signal redundancy. High correlations (>0.8) between signals indicate that they capture similar information, suggesting that simpler fusion strategies (fewer signals) may suffice. Low correlations (<0.3) indicate that signals capture orthogonal aspects of dynamics, justifying multi-signal fusion.

An important design decision is the exposure rather than concealment of intermediate outputs. The pipeline saves tICA coordinates (`tica_coords.npy`), discrete trajectories (`dtraj.npy`), MSM transition matrices (`P.npy`), and per-signal scores (columns in `frame_scores_v2.csv`). This granular output enables users to diagnose failures, identify which signals contribute most to final scores, and modify fusion strategies without rerunning expensive upstream stages. The cost is increased complexity and potential user confusion, but the benefit is auditability: users can verify that the pipeline is behaving sensibly rather than trusting a black-box anomaly score.

## 7. Output Artifacts and Structural Alignment

The pipeline produces a carefully architected collection of output files designed for downstream analysis, visualization, and validation. The design philosophy treats persistence as a feature rather than an implementation detail: by saving intermediate results, the pipeline enables iterative refinement, external validation, and integration with orthogonal methods.

The primary output file `frame_scores_v2.csv` (generated by `scoring/anomaly_v2.py`, lines 393-403) contains per-frame anomaly scores with detailed component breakdowns. Each row corresponds to a trajectory frame, with columns for frame index, raw score (0-100 scale), windowed smoothed score, and normalized component scores for each signal (rarity, transition surprise, local density, and optional energy/pocket signals). This structure enables users to determine not just which frames are anomalous but why: a frame with high rarity but low transition surprise likely represents a thermodynamically rare but kinetically accessible state, whereas high surprise with moderate rarity indicates a barrier-crossing event. The row-wise organization (frames as records) facilitates time-series analysis, correlation with external observables, and alignment with simulation metadata.

The model selection outputs (`reports/vamp2_grid.csv` and `reports/vamp2_best.json`) preserve the full hyperparameter search results. The grid file tabulates VAMP-2 scores for all tested (lag, dim) combinations, enabling retrospective assessment of parameter sensitivity. The best parameters file records not just the optimal values but also the number of candidates tested, validation fraction, and random seed, ensuring reproducibility. This metadata is critical for peer review: a reported "optimal" lag time of 30 frames means little without knowing whether 25 and 35 frames were nearly as good (indicating robustness) or significantly worse (indicating a sharp optimum).

The validation report (`reports/validation_report.json`, generated by `tools/validate_model.py`) aggregates multiple quality metrics into a structured JSON document. The report includes Chapman-Kolmogorov test results (lag times tested, mean and maximum errors, pass/fail status), implied timescales convergence (coefficient of variation, convergence status), stationary distribution accuracy (maximum relative error between MSM and empirical estimates), and cross-validation scores. The overall status field (PASSED/NEEDS_REVIEW/FAILED) provides a high-level summary, but the detailed metrics enable diagnosis of specific issues. A failing CK test with converged implied timescales suggests too-coarse discretization rather than insufficient lag time, guiding remediation.

The bootstrap confidence interval files (`models/msm_bootstrap/pi_ci.parquet`, `P_ci.npz`, `mfpt_ci.parquet`) quantify parameter uncertainty using a compact statistical representation. Rather than saving all 100 bootstrap samples (requiring gigabytes for large MSMs), the implementation saves summary statistics: mean, standard deviation, and confidence interval bounds. The Parquet format for stationary distributions enables efficient filtering and aggregation (e.g., "show me all states with π > 0.01 ± 0.005"), while the compressed NumPy format for transition matrices balances filesize against accessibility.

The tICA model and projection outputs (`models/tica/tica_model.pkl`, `data/derived/tica_coords.npy`) enable external applications to project new trajectories into the learned slow-mode space. This is critical for comparing trajectories from different simulation conditions (mutants, ligand-bound states, different force fields) within a common reference frame. The pickled model includes eigenvectors, eigenvalues, and mean/covariance parameters, allowing full reconstruction of the tICA transform. The coordinates file uses a simple binary format optimized for numerical computing libraries, with shape (n_frames, n_dims) and contiguous memory layout for efficient slicing.

Residue-level outputs (various `*_residue_*.json` files in the analysis module) map frame-level or state-level scores to per-residue contributions. The JSON format is chosen for human readability and compatibility with web-based visualization tools, despite being less space-efficient than binary formats. Each file includes explicit residue IDs (not zero-based array indices) to prevent off-by-one errors when aligning with PDB structures, which may have non-sequential numbering or insertion codes.

The artifact alignment strategy ensures consistency across all outputs. Frame indices are 0-based and consistent with input trajectory frame numbering after accounting for stride. Residue indices match the topology file, preserving non-sequential numbering, insertions, and chain IDs. tICA dimensions are ordered by decreasing timescale (component 0 is slowest), and MSM states are ordered by decreasing stationary probability (state 0 is most populated). These conventions are documented in each file's header or accompanying README, preventing misinterpretation.

An important design decision is the separation of raw scores and normalized scores. The pipeline outputs both unnormalized signal values (in physical units: probabilities for π, energies in kcal/mol for contact energies, distances in Angstroms for k-NN) and normalized dimensionless scores (0-100 scale for visualization). This dual representation serves distinct purposes: raw values enable quantitative comparison with external data or theoretical predictions, while normalized scores facilitate ranking and thresholding for hotspot selection. The separation is maintained throughout the output files, with clear naming conventions (e.g., `energy` vs. `energy_score`) to prevent confusion.

## 8. Failure Modes, Limitations, and Defensive Design

The implementation exhibits uncommon scientific honesty by explicitly documenting known failure modes and designing defensive mechanisms to detect and mitigate them. This section synthesizes evidence from code comments, validation tests, and documentation to catalog situations where hotspot detection may be misleading.

The most fundamental limitation is the Markovian assumption underlying MSM construction. MSMs assume that the probability of transitioning from state i to state j depends only on i, not on the prior history of state visits. This assumption fails when the protein exhibits memory effects: slow processes (e.g., proline isomerization, buried water rearrangement) that have not equilibrated within the MSM lag time. The Chapman-Kolmogorov test (`msm/validation.py`, lines 23-79) diagnoses such failures by checking if P^k matches empirical k-step transitions, but passing the CK test is necessary not sufficient: it only validates the Markov property for the tested lag multiples, not for all processes.

Insufficient sampling represents a pervasive failure mode explicitly tested in `tests/test_pipeline_edge_cases.py` (lines 52-133). For very short trajectories (<100 frames), MSM statistics become unreliable: rare states may be visited once or not at all, transition counts are dominated by sampling noise, and confidence intervals span 0-1 for most parameters. The validation module issues warnings when frames per cluster fall below 100 (line 102 in scientific guide), but the pipeline still produces numerical outputs. Users may interpret these outputs as meaningful without recognizing their statistical fragility. The defensive design choice here is to complete the pipeline with warnings rather than crashing, on the grounds that even poor estimates may be informative in data-scarce regimes, but this relies on users heeding warnings.

Force field artifacts and trajectory quality issues can produce misleading hotspots. If the force field incorrectly stabilizes a non-native state, the MSM will identify it as highly populated (low rarity), yet the biology may be irrelevant. If the trajectory exhibits drifting or progressive unfolding due to inadequate equilibration or system instability, the temporal dimension becomes non-stationary, violating MSM assumptions. The pipeline includes no explicit checks for non-stationarity beyond visual inspection of time-series plots, reflecting an assumption that users will perform standard trajectory validation (RMSD stability, energy conservation, secondary structure maintenance) externally.

Discretization artifacts arise from the k-means clustering step (30 states default). This choice is somewhat arbitrary: too few states (underresolution) merge kinetically distinct basins, while too many states (overresolution) split single basins, diluting transition statistics. The optimal cluster count is system-dependent and unknowable without ground truth. The implementation uses a fixed count rather than adaptive schemes (e.g., elbow method, silhouette analysis) for reproducibility, but documents this as a hyperparameter users should tune. The validation suite includes no systematic test of clustering robustness, though bootstrap sampling provides indirect sensitivity assessment.

The multi-signal fusion strategy assumes independence or at least complementarity between signals, which may fail when signals are highly correlated. If rarity and transition surprise are strongly correlated (both high for rarely visited states with high barriers), median fusion provides little benefit over single-signal scoring. The pipeline computes and saves signal correlation matrices (`reports/signal_correlations.png`, generated by validation module) to diagnose this issue, but provides no automated remediation. Users must interpret correlations and potentially drop redundant signals manually.

Residue-level aggregation from frame-level scores introduces spatial ambiguity. A frame may be anomalous due to a collective motion involving many residues (e.g., domain closure), yet the aggregation may incorrectly attribute the anomaly to the most flexible residue (if using RMSF weighting) rather than the functionally critical hinge. The implementation provides multiple aggregation strategies (weighted-mean, max, mean) without guidance on which to use when, reflecting genuine scientific uncertainty about the "correct" mapping.

Temporal resolution limits arise from the lag times used. The MSM lag (typically 30 frames × 2 ps/frame = 60 ps) means processes faster than ~60 ps are not captured. Fast conformational changes (side-chain rotations, local loop movements) may be functionally important but are averaged over in the MSM, appearing as rapid fluctuations within states rather than state transitions. The pipeline provides no check for such sub-lag processes, though increasing the frame rate (reducing timestep) would mitigate this at computational cost.

The implementation's defensive design patterns include: (1) extensive input validation with informative error messages (`msm/input_validation.py`), catching common mistakes like mismatched topology/trajectory files or reversed parameter values; (2) fallback values for edge cases, e.g., zero-variance normalization defaults to zero scores rather than NaN; (3) verbose logging with progress indicators and intermediate statistics, enabling users to diagnose where failures occur; (4) separation of warnings (logged but non-fatal) from errors (fatal), allowing expert users to override caution while protecting novices; (5) deterministic random seeds for all stochastic operations (k-means clustering, bootstrap sampling), ensuring reproducibility.

Evidence of epistemological skepticism appears throughout the codebase. Comments questioning normalization choices ("rank vs quantile: which is right?"), acknowledging signal correlation issues ("if signals are redundant, fusion is suboptimal"), and noting aggregation ambiguities ("residue contribution is not well-defined for collective motions") reflect an awareness that this pipeline makes methodological choices without definitive ground truth. This skepticism manifests in the design decision to expose rather than hide uncertainty: saving confidence intervals, reporting multiple scores rather than a single number, and documenting failure modes in validation reports.

## Conclusion: Why This Pipeline Cannot Be Treated as a Black Box

This pipeline's architecture embeds dozens of scientific and computational assumptions that critically affect the validity and interpretation of results. The choice of backbone dihedrals as primary features assumes that function arises from backbone rearrangements, which fails for side-chain-driven processes. The VAMP-2 selection criterion assumes that slow modes are functionally important, which fails for fast catalytic motions or rare event sampling. The MSM reversibility constraint assumes equilibrium dynamics, which fails for driven systems or multi-timescale relaxation. The rank normalization assumes that ordinal relationships matter more than absolute magnitudes, which fails when interpreting scores quantitatively. The median fusion assumes that signal failures are independent, which fails for systematic errors affecting multiple signals.

Beyond individual assumptions, the pipeline's integrated nature means that errors propagate and compound. A poor feature choice corrupts tICA, yielding slow modes that do not capture relevant dynamics. Poor slow modes corrupt MSM discretization, yielding states that do not correspond to metastable basins. Poor MSM quality corrupts anomaly signals, yielding rarity scores that reflect sampling artifacts rather than thermodynamics. Validation checks at each stage provide safeguards, but they test necessary not sufficient conditions: passing all checks does not guarantee biological relevance, only methodological self-consistency.

The fundamental irreducibility to a black box stems from the pipeline's role as a hypothesis generator rather than a classifier. Unlike supervised learning where ground truth labels enable validation, unsupervised anomaly detection produces candidates for experimental follow-up, not definitive answers. A residue with a high hotspot score may be functionally critical or statistically spurious; only external validation (mutation studies, binding assays, conservation analysis) can distinguish these cases. Users must therefore engage with the pipeline's internals to assess whether identified hotspots are plausible given the protein's known biology, whether hyperparameters are appropriate given trajectory length and sampling quality, and whether signals are complementary or redundant given their correlation structure.

This necessity for interpretive engagement reflects a mature scientific perspective: computational methods extend human reasoning but do not replace it. The pipeline's value lies not in automating discovery but in systematizing the exploration of high-dimensional dynamics, focusing experimental effort on residues exhibiting unusual kinetic, structural, or energetic behavior. By preserving intermediate outputs, quantifying uncertainties, documenting failure modes, and exposing methodological choices, the implementation enables this interpretive engagement. A black box would be more convenient but less trustworthy; this pipeline's transparency is its strength.
